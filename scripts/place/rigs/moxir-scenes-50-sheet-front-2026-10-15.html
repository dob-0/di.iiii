Eye comfort: the method, written down. No single published metric covers a coloured LED wash, so the method is stated here and its thresholds are marked as taste (unvalidated). It has three parts:

  1. Red flash. A colour counts as “saturated red” when R/(R+G+B) ≥ 0.8, the WCAG 2.2 red-flash state, read here on linear sRGB. Red never strobes. Red never meets green or blue in a moving scene: alternating saturated colour pairs were behind the 1997 Pokémon seizures (Harding et al., Epilepsia 2005; ITU-R BT.1702).
  2. Focus. The eye's lens bends blue more than red: about 1 dioptre between 450 and 589 nm (longitudinal chromatic aberration, Thibos et al., Applied Optics 1992). The centre of the fovea also has almost no blue cones (Wald 1967). So a pure blue or violet floor cannot be held in focus. For each colour the generator computes where the eye must focus, relative to white. It uses the luminance-weighted aberration over RGB LED peaks of 630/525/465 nm (an assumption: no PL5403 spectral data). A floor colour must stay within ±0.2 D, inside the eye's depth of focus of about ±0.3 D (Campbell 1957). For example, pure deep blue is −0.41 D, ember +0.37 D, lavender −0.03 D and petrol −0.10 D.
  3. Night vision. In the dark, blue-green (around 507 nm, the CIE 1951 scotopic peak; mesopic photometry CIE 191:2010) reads brighter than its photopic luminance, and red reads darker. Petrol and deep sea therefore carry the breakdown at low levels. The floor brightness check uses photopic luminance, which is on the safe side for petrol and generous for red.

Palette 2 (the 03:10 audit, kept: the sources of every colour)

“it's only red and amber; I need more underground colours; do the deep audit: look at the place and the mood, the colours of the poster and the vibe; yes red can be, but come on.” (the owner, 15 Oct, 03:10)

How the colours were found. Every clip was decoded at 4 frames a second. Its pixels were split by brightness into glow (the light sources), lit (lit surfaces and haze) and shadow (steel and concrete in the dark). Each group was clustered into 5 colours with k-means in CIE L*a*b* (Lloyd 1982, k-means++ seeding, Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007, seed 0). A second run gave the same numbers to the last digit. Each “measured” line below gives a cluster's median colour and its share. A camera sees a lamp through haze darker and greyer than it is, so the hue is the measured part. The saturation of the lamp colour is taste, and each line says so.

What the place does to light, computed per colour (a matte surface sends back the product of lamp and surface, in linear light). The concrete is a neutral grey: it sends back 43–45% of every colour, so any colour sits on it. Rust sends back red and amber best: 34% of ember and 30% of amber, against 23% of deep blue. Rust is also the hall's own colour: t3's few saturated lights are 56% red and 44% amber, median #a47e6f. The dark steel sends back 2–3% of anything. On the steel, the colour lives in the haze and in the beams, not on the walls. Limit: the surfaces were sampled from a phone video under unknown light and white balance.

Underground practice, the sources named. Cold white beams and strobes in darkness and fog: Berlin techno in the factory and vault years (F. Denk & S. von Thülen, Der Klang der Familie, 2012). Ice/steel blue: in the dark, the eye's rods take over and blue-green reads brighter than red (the Purkinje shift; CIE 1951 scotopic curve, peak 507 nm). UV violet: black light in rave culture (S. Reynolds, Energy Flash, 1998). Acid green: acid house and its lasers (M. Collin, Altered State, 1997). Deep red is the owner's own “cold white / deep red”. Monochrome scenes are a cue tool: one colour per state, the change between states being the event (J. Moody & P. Dexter, Concert Lighting, 4th ed. 2017). Amber is used only as warmth, the sodium street light of the road outside (589 nm). Limit: these books are named from the agent's reading; their page references were not checked in this job.

3 · Published practice. The five acts follow the form of a techno set: intro, build, drop/peak, breakdown, second peak and outro. Source: Mark J. Butler, Unlocking the Groove (Indiana University Press, 2006), on breakdowns and builds in electronic dance music. The cue craft (looks as states, fade and hold per cue, hits kept short, the biggest look saved for the second peak) follows J. Moody & P. Dexter, Concert Lighting (Focal Press, 4th ed. 2017). The moving-light effects are run as offsets from a rest position, as in R. Cadena, Automated Lighting (Focal Press, 2nd ed. 2010). The flash limit is W3C WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.1 (at most three flashes in any one second), the same limit as Ofcom's guidance for broadcast.

The five rules

  1. Dark first. Every scene lights only a few parts. Act 1 and Act 4 stay under 60% on most parts, and no scene is pure black.
  2. Colour arcs, one family per act. Each act has its own colours (see Palette 3), and most scenes use one or two of them. The green of the lasers is diode green. Red is an accent in at most 6 scenes (now 4), never the floor. No amber inside; the dark amber of the entrance is the owner's. No saturated pure blue or violet on any lamp.
  3. A readable floor. In every scene line L and line R hold still in the act's floor colour, dim and desaturated, never red: act 1 ice 0.07; act 2 mint 0.07; act 3 rose-copper 0.14; act 4 petrol 0.08; act 5 lavender 0.15; act 5 ends (scenes 48-50) ice 0.07; blue-wave: cut down lavender 0.3. The one exception is “Blue wave”, where the strips join the wave in petrol and the cut-down washes hold the floor in lavender at 30%. Each floor is at least as bright as the v1 deep-ember floor, and within 0.2 dioptres of white for the eye's focus (both checked by the generator). A lamp at or under 30% never takes part in the motion.
  4. At most 3 flashes a second on any lamp. This was measured on the desk's own engine, the worst second of 2 minutes on 12 lamps, and re-measured for this version with the same result. Glitch is never used (6 flashes at 33 bpm). Sparkle runs only at 20 bpm (3). Strobe is held under 3 by the engine. Strobe and blinder hits last 6–8 s.
  5. The beams lean; they do not spin in place. The 18 beams rest pointing straight up, so turning them (pan) alone shows nothing. Every head motion therefore leans them (tilt), at most 30° off vertical so they stay over people's heads. The patterns are nod, wave, circle and figure 8. Washes and lasers never move their heads. The laser desk cap stays at 0.16.
